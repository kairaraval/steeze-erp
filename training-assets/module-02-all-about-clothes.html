All About Clothes

The vocabulary of a garment. Every part, every cut, every trim — named the way production names them, explained the way a client needs to hear it.

~90 minStudy time Module 01Prerequisite: Techpack in the OS 80%Passing score Sept 2026Version 1.0

Start here

Why a salesperson needs production vocabulary

A client says "I want the collar a bit stiffer and the sleeves to end higher." Production hears "self-fabric collar with fusing, shorten sleeve length by 2 cm." Your job is to be the translator in both directions. When you use the right word, three things happen: the techpack is right the first time, the sample comes back right the first time, and the client trusts that they are talking to a manufacturer, not a reseller.

This module gives you that vocabulary. Read it once end to end, then use it as a reference while you build techpacks. The diagrams are the same views used in our techpack templates, so the numbers here match the callouts you will write.

How to use this module

Each diagram has numbered pins. The legend under it names the part, then tells you the one thing a client usually cares about for that part. Memorize the names; the sales notes come naturally once you have done a few techpacks.

Part 1

Parts of a garment

Five garments cover almost everything Steeze makes: the polo shirt, the t-shirt, the button-down shirt, pants, and the jacket. Learn these five and you can describe any variation — a jersey is a t-shirt with panels, a hoodie is a jacket with a hood and no opening, a jogger is a pair of pants with a rib hem.

The polo shirt

Our highest-volume corporate garment. A polo is defined by three things: a knit collar, a short placket with buttons, and (usually) piqué or interlock fabric. Everything else is an option.

Questions to ask the client

Collar: flat knit or self-fabric? Tipping? · Placket: 2 or 3 buttons, hidden placket? · Sleeve: short or long, rib cuff or plain hem? · Fit: regular or slim, men's/women's/unisex blocks? · Side vents: yes or no? · Contrast panels or piping?

The t-shirt

The simplest garment we make — and the one where fit and fabric are the whole conversation, because there is nothing else to hide behind.

The polo button-down (long-sleeve woven shirt)

Clients say "polo button-down" for a woven shirt with a full button front — what production calls a woven shirt or dress/work shirt. It is a different animal from a knit polo: woven fabric (no stretch), a stand collar, cuffs, a yoke and a full placket. More parts means more decisions.

Common confusion

"Polo button-down" (client language) = a woven shirt with buttons all the way down. "Button-down collar" (production language) = the collar points button to the shirt. A shirt can be one without the other. Confirm which one the client means before the techpack.

Pants

Trousers, slacks, work pants, joggers — all share one anatomy. The words that matter most in a fitting are rise, inseam, and leg opening. Get those three in the techpack and the rest is styling.

The jacket

Jackets are our highest-margin category and the one with the most components. The base drawing is a zip-front bomber: once you can name these parts, a windbreaker, varsity, or track jacket is just a change of fabric, closure, and collar.

Questions to ask the client — jackets

Purpose (rain, cold aircon, uniform look, event giveaway)? · Lined or unlined? · Hood or collar? · Pocket count and type (welt, zip, patch)? · Cuff and hem finish (rib, elastic, plain)? · Zipper type (metal, nylon coil, plastic vislon)? · Logo placements and method (embroidery on chest is typical)?

Part 2

Types & cuts

Clients rarely ask for "a t-shirt." They ask for "the one like the Nike one" or "something not too tight." Your job is to turn that into a named cut. This part gives you the names — and the one-line description that lets you confirm it with the client.

Shirt cuts, necklines and sleeves

Three independent choices define a top: the neckline, the sleeve construction, and the fit. Any neckline can go with any sleeve and any fit — so name all three in the techpack.

Necklines

Crew neck

Round neck with a rib band. The default tee and jersey neckline.

Event tees · Jerseys · Undershirts

V-neck

Pointed neckline. Depth is the variable: shallow for corporate, deep for fashion.

Corporate tees · Women's tops · Scrubs

Polo collar

Knit collar plus short placket. The corporate standard.

Corporate polos · Team polos

Henley

Crew neck with a buttoned placket and no collar.

Casual uniforms · Hospitality

Scoop neck

Wider, deeper round neck. Mostly women's cuts.

Women's tees · Activewear

Mock neck / turtleneck

A short standing rib collar (mock) or a tall folding one (turtle).

Compression · Cold-storage uniforms · Fashion

Mandarin / band collar

Short standing collar with no fold-over points. Neat, modern corporate look.

F&B uniforms · Spa · Clinics

Shirt collar (with stand)

Woven-shirt collar on a stand. Spread, point, or button-down variants.

Button-down shirts · Coach jackets

Sleeve construction and fit

Set-in sleeve

Sleeve sewn into a round armhole. Clean shoulder line; the standard.

Polos · Corporate · Most tees

Raglan sleeve

Seam runs from underarm to neckline. Free shoulder movement; easy to color-block.

Baseball tees · Jerseys · Sportswear

Drop shoulder / oversized

Shoulder seam sits below the shoulder point. Boxy, relaxed silhouette.

Streetwear tees · Merch · Lifestyle line

Long sleeve

Full-length sleeve, hemmed or with rib cuff. Add thumbholes for activewear.

Cold aircon offices · Outdoor crews · Sun protection

Sleeveless / tank / muscle

No sleeve. Muscle tee = tee block with sleeves removed; tank = narrow straps.

Basketball jerseys · Gym · Events

Ringer tee

Contrast rib at neck and sleeve hems. Cheap way to add a brand color.

Retro merch · Fun runs

Slim fit vs regular fit

Slim = 4–6 cm less chest ease, higher armhole. Regular = the dashed line. Always ask.

Fit is a techpack field, not a guess

Longline / curved hem

Extra body length with a rounded hem, sometimes side splits.

Lifestyle line · Streetwear
Sales note

Women's cuts are not "smaller men's cuts." A women's block has a narrower shoulder, shaped side seam, shorter body, and a shorter sleeve. If the client has a mixed team, ask: unisex sizing (one block, S–3XL) or separate men's and women's blocks (better fit, more SKUs to manage).

Types of pants

Pants are named by two things: silhouette (how the leg is shaped) and style (the details). "Slim-fit chino" is a silhouette plus a style. "Tapered jogger" is a silhouette plus a style.

Silhouettes — the leg shape

Straight

Same width from knee to hem. Safe, classic, suits most bodies.

Slacks · Uniform trousers

Slim

Narrower through thigh and leg, not tight. Modern corporate default.

Corporate · Hospitality

Tapered

Roomy at the thigh, narrows to the ankle. The jogger shape.

Joggers · Athleisure

Skinny

Tight through the whole leg. Needs stretch fabric. Rare in uniforms.

Fashion only

Wide / relaxed

Full leg from hip to hem. Comfort and airflow.

Workwear · Kitchen pants · Fashion

Bootcut / flare

Narrow at the knee, opens at the hem. Sits over boots.

Security · Western-style uniforms

Styles — the details

Chino

Cotton twill, slant pockets, flat front, no crease. Smart-casual.

Corporate casual · Retail staff

Dress slacks / trousers

Pressed crease, welt back pockets, formal waistband. Often with pleats.

Corporate · Front office · Banks

Cargo

Large bellows pockets on the thigh. Utility look, lots of storage.

Field crews · Maintenance · Delivery

Jogger

Elastic waistband with drawcord, tapered leg, rib or elastic cuff at the ankle.

Sports · Gym · Casual uniforms

Track pants

Polyester, side stripe/piping, elastic waist, sometimes zip at ankle.

Team warm-ups · Sports uniforms

Shorts

Any style cut above the knee. Inseam 5", 7", 9" are the common lengths.

Sports · Summer uniforms · Kids

Work pants / utility

Heavy twill or canvas, reinforced knees, tool pockets, triple-stitched seams. The Dickies/Carhartt category.

Factories · Construction · Steeze Sports crew

Pleated vs flat front

Pleats (dashed) add room at the hip. Flat front is slimmer. Ask, especially for older or larger teams.

A techpack field

Types of jackets

Jackets are named by their construction — the collar, the closure, and the hem tell you what it is. Here are the ones we sell most, plus the words that separate them.

Bomber

Rib collar, rib cuffs, rib hem, zip front. Cropped to the waist.

Corporate outerwear · Varsity-style · Merch

Hoodie (pullover)

Hood, kangaroo pocket, rib cuffs and hem, no front opening. Fleece or French terry.

Merch · Schools · Lifestyle line

Zip hoodie

Same as pullover but with a full front zipper and split pocket.

Staff layering · Merch

Windbreaker

Light nylon/polyester shell, stand collar or hood, elastic cuffs, drawcord hem. Packable.

Fun runs · Field staff · Rainy-season giveaway

Track jacket

Stand collar, full zip, contrast stripes or piping down the sleeves, rib or elastic hem. Polyester.

Team warm-ups · Sports clubs · Coaches

Varsity / letterman

A bomber with contrast sleeves (often faux leather or fleece), snap front, chenille patch on chest.

Schools · Batch jackets · Fraternities

Coach jacket

Shirt-style collar, snap-button front, nylon shell, drawcord hem, slash pockets.

Streetwear merch · Coaches · Events

Softshell

Bonded stretch polyester with a fleece inner. Windproof, water-resistant, structured. Premium corporate.

Executives · Field engineers · Golf

Puffer / quilted jacket

Quilted channels filled with synthetic padding. Warmth. Also as a vest (sleeveless).

Cold storage · Travel · Corporate gifts

Vest (gilet)

Sleeveless layer: quilted, fleece, or softshell. Popular because it fits over a polo.

Corporate · Golf tournaments · Site staff

Fleece jacket

Polar fleece body, full or half zip, no lining needed. Warm, light, cheap.

BPO night shifts · Aircon offices · Outdoor events

Blazer / suit jacket

Lapels, buttons, fully tailored with lining and shoulder pads. The formal end of corporate.

Front desk · Banks · Airlines · Managers

Part 3

Trims

Trims are everything on a garment that is not the main fabric: zippers, buttons, snaps, rib bands, elastics, cords, labels, tapes, thread. They are a small share of cost and a large share of how "premium" a garment feels — and the most common reason a sample is late, because trims are bought per order.

Zippers

Every zipper has the same anatomy. When a client asks for "a better zipper," you need to know which part they mean.

Buttons, snaps and closures

Knitted bands: collars, cuffs and rib

A flat knit collar is knitted to shape on a collar machine, with a finished edge — it does not fray and it can carry a stripe pattern (tipping). A rib band (or ribbing) is a stretchy knit fabric with vertical ridges, cut and sewn into cuffs, hems, neckbands, and the collar of a bomber.

The rest of the trim list

Every techpack has a trim sheet. These are the lines you will fill in most often:

  • ThreadPolyester spun (standard) or core-spun (strong). Color-matched; contrast for topstitching.
  • Interlining / fusingGlued-on stiffener inside collars, plackets, cuffs, waistbands.
  • Neck tape / shoulder tapeWoven tape covering the inside neck seam. Printable for branding.
  • PipingNarrow folded strip sewn into a seam for a contrast line. Flat or corded.
  • BindingStrip that wraps a raw edge (neck, armhole). Common on jerseys.
  • Twill tape / herringbone tapeFlat woven tape used on plackets, hanging loops, drawcords.
  • Eyelets & grommetsMetal rings for drawcords or ventilation.
  • Cord tips / cord locks (toggles)Ends and stoppers for drawcords.
  • Reflective tapeHeat-applied or sewn strips; safety uniforms, delivery riders.
  • Hanger loop / locker loopSmall loop at the back neck for hanging.
  • Pocket bag fabric (pocketing)TC or polyester lining fabric inside pockets, not self fabric.
  • LiningTaffeta, mesh, or fleece inside a jacket. Full, half, or partial.
  • Shoulder padsBlazers only. Foam or felt.
  • Hook & bar / hook & eyeWaistband closure on slacks; concealed.
  • Buckles, D-rings, slidersBags, aprons, vests.
  • Poly bag, hang tag, stickerPackaging trims. Size sticker on the bag is standard.

Part 4

Collars, plackets, pockets and hems

These are the details a client points at when they say "can we change this part." Each one is a named option in the techpack.

Placket types

Standard polo placket (2-button)

Two-piece placket, ≈ 15 cm. 3-button is ≈ 18 cm. Facing inside is self fabric or contrast.

Hidden (concealed) placket

Buttons covered by a fly strip. Clean look, harder to sew, higher price.

Zip placket (quarter-zip)

Short zipper instead of buttons. Sporty; common on golf polos and pullovers.

Full button front (woven shirt)

Center-front placket the whole length. Standard, French (folded, no visible stitching), or concealed.

Pocket types

Patch pocket

Sewn on top of the garment. Cheapest. Chest pockets, cargo, back pockets.

Welt pocket

A slit with a fabric strip (welt). Double welt = two strips. Slacks, blazers, jackets.

Flap pocket

Patch or welt pocket with a flap over it; button, snap, or velcro. Workwear look.

Bellows (cargo) pocket

Patch pocket with pleated sides so it expands. Cargo pants, utility vests.

Slant / slash pocket

Diagonal opening at the hip. Chinos and jackets. "Quarter-top" = the jeans version.

Kangaroo pocket

Front pouch with two side openings. Hoodies.

Zip pocket

Welt pocket closed with a zipper. Security for phones; jackets and joggers.

Pen pocket / sleeve pocket

Narrow pocket with a pen slot. Engineers, technicians, hospital staff love it.

Hems and finishes

  • Straight hemLevel all around. Standard for polos and tees.
  • Curved / shirttail hemLonger front and back, dips at side. Woven shirts for tucking.
  • Drop-tail hemBack longer than front. Athletic tops and golf polos.
  • Side vents (slits)Short openings at the side seam hem, 4–6 cm. Polos.
  • Rib hemKnit band at the bottom. Bombers, hoodies, joggers.
  • Elastic hem / drawcord hemAdjustable bottom. Windbreakers, coach jackets.
  • Rolled / raw edgeFashion tees only. Not for uniforms.
  • Cuffed hem (turn-up)Pants with the hem folded up on the outside.

Part 5

Stitches and seams

You do not need to sew, but you need to recognize the four stitches that show up in every quality conversation — because "the stitching looks cheap" is the most common complaint about a sample, and it usually has a specific name.

  • Seam allowanceFabric beyond the stitch line, usually 1 cm. Not visible but decides fit.
  • Stitches per inch (SPI)Density. 10–12 SPI is standard; higher = finer, slower, pricier.
  • Princess seam / panel seamVertical shaped seams on the front; women's polos and jerseys.
  • Side panelA separate strip along the side, often mesh or contrast. Jerseys.
  • YokeHorizontal panel across shoulders (shirts) or hip (pants).
  • GussetDiamond of fabric at the crotch or underarm for movement.
  • FacingInside piece that finishes an edge (placket, neckline).
  • PuckeringWrinkled seam from wrong tension — a rejectable defect.

Sales note — sizing

Philippine and Asian sizing runs about one size smaller than US sizing. A client who "wears Large in Uniqlo" may need XL in a US-block polo. Always offer a size set (one of each size) for the client to fit before bulk — it prevents the number-one post-delivery complaint.

Part 7

Labels and packaging

  • Main / brand labelWoven (damask, premium) or printed satin. Sewn at the back neck. Can be the client's brand.
  • Size labelWoven or printed; often combined with the main label.
  • Care labelPrinted satin or nylon; wash symbols and fabric content. Required for retail.
  • Heat-transfer (tagless) labelPrinted directly on the fabric. No itch; premium feel; needs the right ink for polyester.
  • Hang tagCard tag with string or plastic swift-tack. Branding for retail or gifting.
  • Poly bagOne garment per bag, size sticker outside. Fold standard: 25 × 35 cm.
  • CartonMaster packing: assorted or solid size per carton. Packing list on the outside.

Reference

Quick glossary

  • Block / patternThe base template a garment is cut from. "Men's polo block."
  • GradingScaling a pattern up and down through the size range.
  • EaseExtra room beyond body measurement. Slim = less ease.
  • Self fabricThe main body fabric, used again for a part (self-fabric collar).
  • ContrastA different color or fabric used for a part.
  • Color-blockingPanels in different colors.
  • TippingStripe knitted into the edge of a collar or cuff.
  • FusingHeat-bonded stiffener inside a part.
  • TopstitchVisible stitching on the outside, functional or decorative.
  • Drop shoulderShoulder seam sits below the natural shoulder.
  • HPSHigh Point Shoulder — the measuring origin for body length.
  • SweepWidth of the hem.
  • RiseWaistband to crotch on pants.
  • Inseam / outseamInside leg / outside leg length.
  • GussetExtra fabric piece for movement.
  • Storm flapFabric strip covering a zipper against wind and rain.
  • Ligne (L)Button size unit. 18L polo standard.
  • SPIStitches per inch.
  • Size setOne sample of each size for fitting before bulk.
  • Pre-production sample (PPS)The final approved sample bulk is checked against.

Knowledge check

Module 02 quiz

Twelve questions. Pass mark is 80% (10 of 12). Retake as many times as you like — the goal is fluency, not the score.

01A client wants their logo "on the chest, the usual side." Where does it go?

Standard logo placement is the wearer's left chest — which appears on the RIGHT when you look at the shirt. Always say "wearer's left" in the techpack.

02Which part is a polo's "placket"?

The placket is the buttoned opening. Standard is 2 or 3 buttons, about 15–18 cm long.

03A raglan sleeve is identified by…

Raglan = diagonal seam to the neck. Drop shoulder = seam below the shoulder. Set-in = round armhole at the shoulder.

04On pants, the "rise" is measured from…

Rise = waistband to crotch. Waist to hem on the outside is the outseam; crotch to hem inside is the inseam.

05What makes a jacket a bomber?

Hood + kangaroo pocket = hoodie. Shirt collar + snaps = coach jacket. Rib everything + zip = bomber.

06A client asks for a "polo button-down." Before you write the techpack you should confirm…

"Polo button-down" is ambiguous. The woven shirt and the knit polo are different garments with different fabrics, parts, and prices.

07Zipper "#5" refers to…

The number is the approximate chain width in mm. #5 is the jacket standard; #3 for pockets; #8–#10 for bags and heavy coats.

08Which stitch gives a tee hem its two parallel rows on the outside and stretch?

Coverstitch (double needle) is the knit hem stitch. Overlock finishes raw edges inside; lockstitch is the plain straight stitch.

09A flat knit collar differs from a self-fabric collar because it…

Flat knit collars are knitted separately on a collar machine. Self-fabric collars are cut from the same fabric as the body and usually fused for structure.

10Body length is measured from…

HPS — where the shoulder seam meets the collar — is the standard origin for body length.

11A welt pocket is…

Patch = sewn on top. Bellows = pleated sides. Welt = slit with a strip.

12A jogger is defined by…

Crease + welt = slacks. Bellows pockets = cargo. Elastic waist + taper + cuff = jogger.
