Print Techniques

How a logo gets onto a garment. Six methods, how each one works, what it needs from the fabric, and how to pick the right one for the client in front of you.

~60 minStudy time Module 02Prerequisite: All About Clothes 80%Passing score Sept 2026Version 1.0

Start here

The decoration decides the fabric, the price and the lead time

A client's logo is the emotional center of every order. The method we use to put it on the garment is usually the biggest single driver of unit cost after fabric, and it locks in what fabric we can use. If you recommend embroidery on a thin dri-fit, the sample will pucker. If you promise sublimation on a cotton tee, the print will wash out. Learning this module means never making those two promises.

The one rule

Sublimation only works on polyester (light colors). Everything else in this module exists because of that rule — either to decorate polyester in a different way, or to decorate fabrics sublimation can't touch.

Decision guide

How to choose a method in 30 seconds

Ask three questions in this order: What fabric? How big and how many colors is the artwork? How many pieces? The diagram gives you the usual answer; the rest of the module tells you why.

Method 1

Full sublimation

Sublimation is a dye process, not a print on top of the fabric. Special ink is printed onto transfer paper, then heat-pressed against polyester at about 200 °C. The ink turns into gas, the polyester fibers open, and the dye bonds inside the fiber. When it cools, the color is part of the fabric — you cannot feel it, and it will not crack or peel.

Full sublimation (also "all-over" or "full-print") means every panel of the garment is printed before it is sewn. The design can cover the whole garment, seam to seam, in unlimited colors, for no extra cost per color.

What it's good at

  • Unlimited colors, gradients, photos, patterns — same price as one color.
  • Zero hand-feel, zero cracking, zero peeling. Breathability of the fabric is untouched.
  • Names and numbers on jerseys are printed at the same time, no extra step.
  • Whole-garment branding: side panels, sleeves, collars can all carry the design.

What it can't do

  • Cotton, or any blend under ~85% polyester. The dye has nothing to bond to; the print looks faded and washes out.
  • Dark or black base fabric. Sublimation has no white ink — it can only darken. Design a black jersey by printing black; do not start from black fabric.
  • Pure white areas need care. White = unprinted fabric, so any dust or shifting during pressing shows up there ("ghosting").
  • Seams and folds can leave thin white lines if panels shift — an accepted tolerance at armholes, not on a logo.

Cost and lead-time drivers

Paper and ink are priced per square metre, so a full-sublimation garment costs the same whether it is one color or fifty. Cost goes up with garment size and count of panels, not with design complexity. Lead time depends on layout: every size needs its own artwork file, so a 7-size run is 7 layouts before printing starts.

How to sell it

"Your design becomes the fabric — nothing on top to peel. Any number of colors, no extra cost. It needs to be polyester and we build the color from white, so let's look at our poly fabrics for this." Ideal: sports jerseys, team kits, fun-run shirts, all-over corporate polos, e-sports, cycling.

Method 2

Spot sublimation

Same chemistry, smaller area. Spot sublimation applies the transfer to one location on a finished or pre-cut garment — a chest logo, a back print, a sleeve — instead of printing every panel. It is the fast, cheap way to get a full-color logo on a white or light polyester garment.

Watch out

On a colored polyester (say, royal blue), spot sublimation still works — but the colors mix with the blue. A yellow logo comes out green. Only promise true colors on white or very light fabric; on colored fabric, recommend DTF, vinyl or embroidery.

Method 3

Embroidery

The logo is stitched into the fabric with thread. The artwork is first digitized — converted into a stitch file that tells the machine where every stitch goes — then the garment is hooped with a backing (stabilizer) behind it and the machine sews it. Embroidery is the corporate standard because it reads as permanent and expensive, and because it works on almost any fabric with enough body.

The vocabulary

  • Stitch countNumber of stitches in the design. The main price driver. A left-chest logo is typically 5,000–10,000 stitches.
  • DigitizingOne-time conversion of artwork into a stitch file. Charged per design; kept on file for reorders.
  • Satin stitchLong zig-zag stitches for outlines, text, borders. Shiny.
  • Fill (tatami) stitchRows of short stitches for large areas. Flatter, matte.
  • Running stitchSingle line; fine details and underlay.
  • UnderlayHidden stitches under the design that stabilize the fabric.
  • Backing / stabilizerPaper-like sheet behind the fabric. Cut-away (permanent, knits) or tear-away (wovens).
  • ToppingWater-soluble film on top of piqué or fleece so stitches don't sink into the texture.
  • 3D / puff embroideryFoam under satin stitches for a raised effect. Caps, varsity letters.
  • AppliquéA fabric shape stitched down with a satin border. Big letters cheaply.
  • ThreadPolyester (durable, colorfast — our default) or rayon (shinier, weaker). Metallic available.
  • RegistrationHow well layers line up. Bad registration = gaps between outline and fill.

What it's good at / not good at

Good: polos, jackets, caps, woven shirts, bags, fleece — any fabric with weight. Lasts the life of the garment. Reads premium. Not good: very thin knits (dri-fit under 140 gsm puckers), tiny text (under 5 mm the letters close up), gradients and photos (thread is solid color), very large areas (a 30 cm full-back logo is 80,000+ stitches — heavy, stiff and expensive).

How to sell it

"Stitched, not printed — it outlasts the shirt and it's what banks and hotels use." Then manage the artwork: ask for the logo in vector, warn that small text may need to be enlarged or dropped, and quote by stitch count. Reorders are cheaper because digitizing is already paid.

Method 4

DTF (direct-to-film)

DTF prints the design onto a clear PET film using pigment inks with a white underbase, dusts the wet ink with hot-melt adhesive powder, cures it, and then heat-presses the film onto the garment. The film is peeled away and the design stays. Because it carries its own white layer, DTF works on any color and almost any fabric — cotton, polyester, blends, nylon, even leather.

Good at / not good at

  • Full color, gradients, fine detail, photographic — on cotton, on black, on blends. This is the answer when a client wants a colorful logo on a dark cotton tee.
  • No setup cost and no minimum; one piece costs about the same as one hundred per unit. Great for samples and small runs.
  • Durability is good (50+ washes when applied right) but it sits on the fabric: a large solid print can feel like a thin sticker and will not breathe.
  • Big solid areas can crack over time on very stretchy fabric; avoid huge DTF blocks on spandex-heavy knits.
How to sell it

"Any colors, any fabric, no minimum." Position DTF for cotton merch, dark garments, small runs, and complex logos that embroidery would butcher. For a large run of a 1–2 color design on cotton, mention screen printing as the cheaper alternative.

Method 5

Vinyl (heat-transfer vinyl, HTV)

Vinyl is a sheet of colored polyurethane film with adhesive on one side. A cutting plotter cuts the design out of the sheet, the excess is weeded away by hand, and the shape is heat-pressed onto the garment. One sheet = one solid color. Multi-color designs are layered, one color at a time.

Good at / not good at

  • The cleanest way to do names and numbers: each piece is different anyway, and vinyl needs no setup.
  • Solid, crisp edges; exact brand color matching by picking the sheet; specialty finishes (reflective, glitter, flock).
  • Not for more than 2–3 colors (each is a separate layer and a separate press), not for fine detail (weeding tiny pieces is slow and fails), not for large areas (heavy, doesn't breathe).
  • Durability: good on cotton and poly; layers can lift at the edges after many washes if applied badly.
How to sell it

Use vinyl for personalization (names, numbers, department tags), reflective strips on delivery and security uniforms, and simple one-color logos on small runs. If the artwork has more than two colors or any gradient, move the client to DTF.

Method 6

Embroidered sublimation patch

A hybrid that gives you full-color detail and the premium look of stitching. The logo is sublimated onto a piece of white polyester twill (the patch), then a merrow or satin-stitched border is embroidered around the edge. The finished patch is attached to the garment — sewn on, heat-sealed (with an adhesive backing), or with hook-and-loop so it can be removed.

Why it exists

Some logos are too detailed to embroider (small text, gradients, photographs, many colors) but the client still wants a badge that looks stitched and sits on a dark or non-polyester garment. The patch solves all three problems at once: color from sublimation, edge from embroidery, and a base fabric of our choosing.

  • Works on any garment: black cotton, nylon windbreakers, fleece, caps, bags, even leather.
  • Cheaper than dense direct embroidery for detailed logos; consistent across every piece because the patch is made separately.
  • Removable with hook-and-loop — useful for security, medical, and rental uniforms where names change.
  • Limits: it is a distinct raised object; it does not suit thin dri-fit tees; very small patches (under 4 cm) lose the border detail.
How to sell it

"A stitched badge with your full-color logo — on any garment." Offer it when the client's logo has small text or a gradient, when they want one badge across a mixed set (polos, jackets, caps), or when names need to be swapped.

Bonus

Other methods you will hear about

  • Screen printing (silkscreen)Ink pushed through a stencil mesh, one screen per color. Cheapest per piece on big runs of 1–3 colors on cotton; setup per color, so bad for small runs. Plastisol (thick, opaque) or water-based (soft, for light fabrics).
  • Rubberized / silicone printThick raised screen-print effect. Tactile logos on sportswear; also silicone grip tape inside waistbands.
  • Heat-transfer labelTagless size and care information printed on the inside neck. Same family as DTF/vinyl.
  • Woven patch / woven labelThe design is woven with thread on a loom — very fine detail, flat. Brand labels and premium badges.
  • Chenille patchFuzzy looped-yarn letters. Varsity jackets.
  • Laser engraving / etchingBurns a tone-on-tone pattern into polyester fleece or softshell. Subtle luxury look.
  • Debossing / embossingHeat-pressed relief into neoprene, PU leather, or thick fleece. No ink.
  • Reflective transferHeat-applied reflective film (vinyl family) for riders and night crews.
  • DTG (direct-to-garment)Inkjet printing straight onto cotton. Soft, photographic, no minimum — but slower and weaker than DTF on blends. We quote DTF instead.

Quick answers to the questions clients actually ask

  • "Which one lasts the longest?"Sublimation and embroidery. Both are part of the garment.
  • "Which one is cheapest?"Depends on quantity. Small runs: DTF or vinyl. Big runs on cotton: screen print. Big runs on polyester: sublimation.
  • "Can you do my gradient logo?"Yes — sublimation, DTF or a sublimated patch. Not embroidery, not vinyl.
  • "Can you print on black?"DTF, vinyl, embroidery, patch: yes. Sublimation: only if we print the black.
  • "Can you do it on cotton?"Everything except sublimation.
  • "Will it feel like a sticker?"Sublimation and embroidery: no. DTF and vinyl: a little, especially large areas.

Knowledge check

Module 03 quiz

Twelve questions. Pass mark 80%.

01A client wants a full-color gradient logo on a black cotton t-shirt, 40 pieces. Best method?

Sublimation can't do cotton or black. Vinyl can't do gradients. DTF does full color on any fabric with no minimum.

02Why does sublimation need polyester?

Sublimation dye turns to gas and enters opened polyester fibers. Cotton doesn't open, so the dye sits on top and washes out.

03Full sublimation vs spot sublimation — the main difference is…

Same chemistry, different scope. Both need light polyester.

04The main price driver for embroidery is…

Machine time is stitches. A bigger or denser logo = more stitches = higher price. Colors matter much less.

05Which is a poor candidate for embroidery?

Thin knits pucker and text under 5 mm closes up. Suggest spot sublimation or DTF instead — or enlarge the text.

06A jersey order needs a different name and number on each piece, 2 solid colors. Cheapest clean method on polyester?

Personalization = no repeat = no setup value. Vinyl needs no setup; sublimated jerseys print names at no extra step. Screen print needs a screen per unique piece.

07What is "digitizing"?

A one-time fee per design; the file is reused for reorders.

08An embroidered sublimation patch is…

Color from sublimation, edge from embroidery, works on any garment.

09A yellow logo spot-sublimated on a royal-blue polyester polo will come out…

Sublimation has no white ink and only darkens. True colors need a white or very light base.

10Which file should you ask for when the logo will be cut in vinyl?

The plotter needs cut lines, which only vector provides.

11Standard left-chest logo width is about…

7–10 cm wide, 7–9 cm below the shoulder seam. Always write the cm in the techpack.

12A client orders 500 cotton tees with a one-color logo. The most economical method is…

Big run + few colors + cotton = screen print. Its setup cost disappears across 500 pieces.
