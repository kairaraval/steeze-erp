Purchasing Operations & Best Practice

Module 04 taught you how to run a request through to a received delivery. This one is the judgment around it — choosing and managing suppliers, checking what arrives, keeping stock honest, buying at the right price, and the discipline that keeps purchasing and accounting in lockstep.

RolePurchasing Admin PrerequisiteModule 04 Approvals go toCEO Read time~22 min

00 — Orientation

Knowing the steps isn't the job

Module 04 showed you the mechanics: how a Purchase Request becomes a Purchase Order, how to receive it, and how the money is handed to Accounting. Anyone can click those buttons. What makes purchasing good — or expensive — is the judgment around each click: Is this the right supplier? Is the price fair? Do we already have this in stock? Did what arrived actually match the order?

This module is that judgment. Read it once through, then keep it open as a reference for your first month. Every section ends with the habit it's trying to build.

The one-line summary

Buy the right thing, from the right supplier, at the right price, only when stock really needs it — and never let money move until the paperwork matches.

01 — Suppliers

Supplier management

You can't buy well without knowing your suppliers cold. The single most valuable thing you maintain is an up-to-date supplier directory — not in your head, in the OS Suppliers list, so it survives when you're out or when you hand over.

For every supplier we buy from regularly, you should know:

What to trackWhy it matters
What they supplySo you request from the right one without asking around. Group by material — fabric, thread, trims, sublimation paper, ink, packaging.
Lead timeDays from PO to delivery. This is what tells you how early to order. A 7-day lead time on a fabric means the stockout warning has to fire 7+ days before the cut date.
MOQMinimum order quantity. If the MOQ is one roll but the job needs half, you need to know before you promise the margin.
Price listCurrent unit price per item, and the date you last confirmed it. Prices move — an old price in your head loses money.
Payment termsCOD, 50% down, 15/30 days. This drives cashflow and what Accounting has to prepare.
Reliability notesWho delivers on time, who short-ships, who sends the wrong color. Honest notes here save future projects.
Habit

Every critical material should have a backup supplier on file. The day your main fabric supplier is out of stock mid-rush is the wrong day to start searching for a second source.

Vetting a new supplier

Don't send a real order to a supplier you've never used. Qualify them first:

  1. Get a quotation and a sample

    Price in writing, plus a physical sample of the exact material. For fabric, confirm composition and GSM. For ink or paper, confirm it's compatible with our printers.

  2. Confirm the commercials

    MOQ, lead time, and payment terms in writing. A great price with a 30-day lead time may be useless for our turnaround.

  3. Check they're a real, registered business

    Ask for their business details and the ability to issue an official receipt / invoice — Accounting needs this to pay them and to file it correctly.

  4. Start small

    First order should be a small, non-urgent one. Prove they deliver on time and on spec before you route a rush production order through them.

  5. Add them to the OS

    Only after they've passed do they go into the Suppliers list with all their details, so the next PO to them is one click.

Watch out

A new supplier with an unusually low price is a reason to be more careful, not less. Cheap-but-wrong GSM, cheap-but-late, or cheap-but-can't-issue-a-receipt all cost more than they save.

02 — Receiving

Receiving & quality check

Receiving is not "the box arrived." It's the checkpoint where bad stock gets caught before it reaches production and before we pay for it. Every delivery is checked against two documents: the PO (what we ordered) and the supplier's delivery receipt / invoice (what they say they sent).

  1. Count it

    Physical count against the PO quantity. Rolls, pieces, kilos — whatever the unit is. Don't sign for 50 because the DR says 50; count to 50.

  2. Inspect quality

    Right material, right color, right GSM, no damage or defects. For fabric, check the dye lot is consistent. For sublimation paper and ink, check it's the correct type for our machines.

  3. Match to the PO in the OS

    Receive against the Purchase Order so stock goes up by exactly what arrived — not what was ordered. If 8 of 10 rolls came, receive 8.

  4. Handle the gap

    Short delivery, wrong item, or damaged goods: note it on the DR, tell the supplier the same day, and don't let Accounting pay for what we didn't get or can't use.

SituationWhat you do
Exact matchReceive full Count = PO = DR. Receive, update stock, pass to Accounting for payment.
Partial deliveryReceive partial Receive what came. Keep the PO open for the balance, or close and re-order if the supplier can't complete.
Wrong / defectiveReject Don't receive into stock. Document it, return to supplier, and hold payment for that portion.
Over-deliveryConfirm first Don't just absorb extra. Confirm with the supplier whether it's billed before accepting it into stock.
Why this is strict

Once stock is received in the OS, production plans against it and Accounting pays for it. A sloppy receive creates a phantom roll that production expects and a payment for goods we never used. Catch it at the door.

03 — Stock discipline

Reorder points & keeping stock honest

Most stockouts aren't bad luck — they're a reorder that happened too late. The fix is to decide, in advance, the stock level at which you buy again.

Par levels & safety stock

For materials we use on almost every job — common fabric bases, staple thread colors, standard packaging — set a par level: the minimum quantity that should always be on hand. When stock drops to the par level, you reorder, even if no specific project is asking yet. The safety stock buried in that par level covers the supplier's lead time plus a buffer for surprises.

Rule of thumb

Reorder point ≈ (how much we use per day × supplier lead time in days) + a safety buffer. The longer or less reliable the lead time, the bigger the buffer.

FIFO — first in, first out

Use the oldest stock first. Fabric can yellow, crease, or gather dust; dye lots drift over time. Issuing newest-first leaves old rolls aging in the corner until they're unusable. Store and label so the oldest is the easiest to reach.

Label & store so stock = reality

Every item findable, every roll labeled with what it is and when it arrived. The OS stock number is only useful if it matches the shelf. A monthly or rolling cycle count — physically counting a slice of inventory and reconciling it against the OS — keeps the two honest.

Dead stock

Flag materials that haven't moved in a long time — leftover specialty fabric from a one-off order, a discontinued color. Dead stock is money sitting on a shelf. Surface it so we can use it on a future job, return it, or stop reordering it.

Habit

Walk the stock room weekly, not just when something runs out. You'll catch low par levels, mislabeled rolls, and dead stock before they become a problem on a live project.

04 — Price

Price benchmarking & negotiation

Buying well isn't paying the lowest sticker price — it's paying a fair price for the right spec, delivered on time. But you can't know what's fair without comparison.

  • Get more than one quote on anything significant. For a big fabric buy or a new material, two or three quotes tell you the market. One quote tells you nothing.
  • Track price history. Keep the last confirmed price per item. When a supplier quotes higher than last time, you'll see it — and you can ask why instead of just paying it.
  • Negotiate the whole deal, not just the unit price. Payment terms, free or cheaper delivery, a price break at a slightly higher quantity, holding a price for the quarter — these are all worth as much as a few pesos off the unit.
  • Consolidate orders. Buying a month of a staple material in one PO often beats the price — and the delivery hassle — of five small ones. Balance this against cashflow and storage.
  • Don't single-source by habit. Having a second qualified supplier for key materials keeps the main one honest on price and protects you when they're out.
Watch out

A low price that forces a big MOQ you can't use, long lead times, or poor quality isn't a saving. Compare the total cost to the job — spec, timing, waste, and cash tied up — not just the number on the quote.

05 — Money

The 3-way match with Accounting

This is the discipline that protects the company from overpaying, double-paying, or paying for goods we never got. Before any supplier is paid, three documents must agree:

Purchase Orderwhat we ordered
=
Delivery Receiptwhat arrived
=
Supplier Invoicewhat they bill
→
Payonly if all match

If the PO says 10 rolls at ₱X, the DR confirms 10 rolls arrived, and the invoice bills 10 rolls at ₱X — pay it. If any of the three disagree, payment waits until it's resolved. Your job is to be the one who notices the mismatch before the money leaves.

Terms & cashflow

Payment terms aren't just a number — they shape the company's cash. COD and large down-payments drain cash immediately; 15- or 30-day terms give breathing room. When you set up a PO, record the terms and expected pay date so Accounting can plan, and so nothing slips into "overdue" and sours a supplier relationship.

Where you hand off

Purchasing proves what is owed and that it's correct (the match). Accounting handles paying it. A clean handoff is a complete PO, a received DR, and the supplier invoice attached — so Accounting never has to chase you for a document.

06 — Authority

Approval authority — who can say yes

Purchasing spends the company's money, so spending is controlled. The rule at Steeze is simple and absolute:

The rule

Every purchase that commits money is approved by the CEO. There is no amount — however small — that anyone else, including the AGM, decides on their own. Purchasing prepares and recommends; the CEO approves the spend.

In practice that means you build the Purchase Request with the right quantities, the right supplier, and the right price, and route it for approval. You don't raise a PO or commit a supplier until the approval is in. "I was sure it would be approved" is not approval.

When it's urgent

Rush jobs happen. The escalation path is still through the CEO — you just flag it clearly as time-critical with the deadline and why, so it can be approved quickly. Speed comes from making the request easy to say yes to, not from skipping the approval.

07 — When things go wrong

Troubleshooting / FAQ

The page you'll use most in your first month. These are the real situations and the right first move.

What happenedFirst move

Supplier is delayedConfirm the new date in writing. If it misses the cut date, switch to the backup supplier or escalate. Never let a supplier's silence become your surprise — chase proactively.

08 — Integrity

Conflict of interest

Purchasing is a position of trust — you decide where the company's money goes. A few lines that protect both you and Steeze:

  • No kickbacks or personal gifts in exchange for giving a supplier business. Choose suppliers on price, quality, and reliability — nothing else.
  • Disclose any relationship. If a supplier is a friend or relative, say so up front. They can still be used if they're genuinely the best option — but the decision has to be transparent, not hidden.
  • Don't steer orders to a single supplier for personal reasons, or split orders to stay under anyone's notice.
  • Keep the trail clean. Quotes, POs, DRs, and invoices all recorded. If every decision can be explained later, you're doing it right.
The test

If you'd be uncomfortable explaining a purchasing decision to the CEO out loud, don't make it that way. Transparency is the whole job.

09 — Measurement

How the role is measured

You'll know you're doing the job well when these stay healthy. None of them is about buying the cheapest — they're about keeping production fed, money controlled, and stock honest.

MeasureWhat good looks like
Material availabilityProduction rarely or never waits on materials you were responsible for ordering. Stockouts are the headline number.
On-time orderingPOs raised early enough to beat lead times — not firefighting at the deadline.
Price & savingsFair prices confirmed against the market; price creep caught and queried.
Inventory accuracyOS stock matches the shelf at cycle counts. Few surprises, little dead stock.
Clean paperworkEvery payment backed by a matching PO, DR, and invoice. Accounting doesn't chase you.
The mindset

Good purchasing is quiet. When it's working, nobody notices — production never stops, money is controlled, and stock is always right. That invisibility is the win.

11 — Check yourself

Knowledge check

Eight questions. 80% to pass. If you miss one, the correct answer and the reason show — review that section and retake.

Q1A supplier quotes a price much lower than anyone else. What's the right instinct?

A low price is a reason for more diligence, not less. Wrong spec, long lead time, or no official receipt can cost far more than the saving.

Q2The DR says 10 rolls; you count 8. What do you receive in the OS?

Always receive the real count. Receiving 10 creates a phantom roll production expects and a payment for goods we never got.

Q3What triggers a reorder for a staple material?

Par levels let you reorder before a stockout, covering the supplier's lead time. Waiting for empty guarantees production waits.

Q4Before a supplier is paid, which three documents must agree?

The 3-way match — PO = DR = invoice — protects against overpaying, double-paying, or paying for goods we never received.

Q5The AGM asks you to buy ₱3,000 of trims for a rush job. Who approves the spend?

There is no amount anyone else decides alone. Flag it as urgent so it's approved fast, but the approval still comes from the CEO.

Q6A relative owns a supplier that's genuinely a good option. What do you do?

They can be used if they're truly the best option — but only with full disclosure. The decision has to be transparent, not hidden.

Q7Why use FIFO for fabric?

First in, first out stops old rolls aging in the corner until they're unusable.

Q8A PR shows a shortfall of 20 meters on a job. What do you buy?

Buy the gap, not the whole requirement — over-buying ties up cash and shelf space and is how double-ordering happens.
